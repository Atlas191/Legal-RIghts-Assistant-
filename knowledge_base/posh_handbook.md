# POSH Act — workplace sexual harassment (from the MWCD Handbook)

## Handbook 1.3 — Legal basis and the Act (incl. Section 3(1) of the Act)
- source: MWCD Handbook 1.1-1.3, Section 2 opening | topic: workplace | act_section_quoted: 3(1)
- keywords: POSH, Vishaka guidelines, safe workplace, right to equality
Section 3(1) of the Act: "No woman shall be subjected to sexual harassment at any workplace." All workplaces in India are mandated to provide a safe working environment free from sexual harassment for all women. The Act follows the Supreme Court's Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) guidelines and rests on Articles 14, 15 and 21 of the Constitution. It has three elements: prohibition, prevention and redress. The Act gives a civil remedy and is in addition to other laws, so a woman may pursue both civil and criminal proceedings.

## Handbook 2.1 — Who is an aggrieved woman
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.1 | topic: workplace | keywords: who is covered, domestic worker, intern, contract worker, student, visitor
Every woman working in or visiting a workplace is protected, irrespective of age or employment status: regular, temporary, ad hoc or daily wage; engaged directly or through an agent or contractor, with or without the principal employer's knowledge; paid, voluntary or otherwise; express or implied terms. This includes co-workers, contract workers, probationers, trainees, apprentices, domestic workers (a woman working in a dwelling place or house), and students.

## Handbook 2.2 — What is a workplace
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.2 | topic: workplace | keywords: workplace, office transport, business trip, unorganised sector, home
A workplace is "any place visited by the employee arising out of or during the course of employment, including transportation provided by the employer for undertaking such a journey". It covers organised and unorganised sectors and Indian or foreign companies with a place of work in India. It includes: government organisations, companies, corporations and cooperative societies; private organisations, ventures, societies, trusts, NGOs and service providers (commercial, vocational, educational, sports, professional, entertainment, industrial, health or financial activities); hospitals and nursing homes; sports institutes and facilities; places visited during work, including travel and employer-provided transport; and a dwelling place or house.
Unorganised sector means any enterprise owned by an individual or self-employed workers producing or selling goods or providing services, or any enterprise employing fewer than 10 workers.

## Handbook 2.3 — Definition of sexual harassment at the workplace
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.3 | topic: workplace_sexual_harassment | keywords: what counts as sexual harassment, unwelcome behaviour, remarks, pornography, touching
"Sexual harassment" includes any one or more of these unwelcome acts or behaviour, directly or by implication: (1) physical contact or advances; (2) a demand or request for sexual favours; (3) making sexually coloured remarks; (4) showing pornography; (5) any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature.

## Handbook 2.4 — Key elements: unwelcome, sexual, subjective, impact, power
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.4 | topic: workplace_sexual_harassment | keywords: intent vs impact, single incident, power imbalance, welcome vs unwelcome
Workplace sexual harassment is behaviour that is unwelcome, sexual in nature, experienced subjectively, judged by impact and not intent, and often occurs in a matrix of power. A woman may experience a single incident or a series over time, and each case is examined in its own context. Unwelcome behaviour feels bad, is one-sided, makes her feel powerless, is power-based, unwanted, demeaning and causes anger, sadness or loss of self-esteem; welcome behaviour is reciprocal, wanted and between equals. The Delhi High Court (Dr. Punita K. Sodhi v. Union of India, 2010) endorsed analysing harassment from the complainant's perspective.

## Handbook 2.5 — Examples of behaviour that constitute sexual harassment
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.5 | topic: workplace_sexual_harassment | keywords: examples, jokes, messages, whatsapp, stalking, touching, persistent asking out, blackmail
Making sexually suggestive remarks or innuendos; serious or repeated offensive remarks such as teasing about body or appearance; offensive comments or jokes; inappropriate questions or remarks about a person's sex life; displaying sexist or offensive pictures, posters, MMS, SMS, WhatsApp or e-mails; intimidation, threats or blackmail around sexual favours; threats or retaliation against someone who speaks up; unwelcome social invitations with sexual overtones; unwelcome sexual advances with or without promises or threats; physical contact such as touching or pinching; caressing, kissing or fondling against her will (could be assault); invasion of personal space such as cornering or brushing against; persistently asking someone out after being turned down; stalking; abuse of authority to threaten a person's job or performance for sexual favours; falsely accusing and undermining a person behind closed doors for sexual favours; controlling a person's reputation by rumour-mongering about her private life.

## Handbook 2.5 (cont.) — Behaviour that may indicate underlying harassment and merits inquiry, and behaviour that may not be harassment
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.5 | topic: workplace_sexual_harassment
May merit inquiry: public criticism or humiliation; exclusion from group activities or assignments without valid reason; statements damaging reputation or career; unjustified removal of responsibilities; too little or too much work; constant overruling; unjustified monitoring; constant blaming; demeaning jobs outside regular duties; insults and isolation; sabotaging work conditions; smear campaigns; arbitrary disciplinary action; withholding time, budget, autonomy or training.
May not be sexual harassment: following up on work absences; requiring performance to job standards; normal exercise of management rights; work-related stress such as deadlines; conditions of work; constructive feedback about the work mistake and not the person.

## Handbook 2.6 — Two forms: quid pro quo and hostile work environment
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.6 | topic: workplace_sexual_harassment | keywords: quid pro quo, this for that, hostile environment, threat to job
Quid pro quo ("this for that"): an implied or explicit promise of preferential or detrimental treatment in employment, or an implied or express threat about present or future employment status. Hostile work environment: creating a hostile, intimidating or offensive work environment, or humiliating treatment likely to affect her health or safety.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: call centre colleague threatens to spread rumour (quid pro quo)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 A, Example 1 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: quid pro quo
A team leader stays late with a colleague, who proposes that she spend the night with him. She refuses. The next evening he repeats the request and threatens to tell everyone that she made a pass at him if she does not give in. Handbook view: the threat to smear her character in return for refusing a sexual favour is quid pro quo harassment; his behaviour is unwelcome, sexual and harmful.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: domestic worker and male household member (quid pro quo)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 A, Example 2 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: quid pro quo | keywords: domestic worker, employer's home
A domestic worker is left alone with a male member of the household who leers at her, walks around in a towel, and pinches her; when she says she will complain, he threatens to accuse her of stealing and get her fired. Handbook view: threatening her to keep quiet about unwelcome physical contact if she wants to keep her job is quid pro quo harassment in a matrix of power.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: director forces himself on researcher during field visit (quid pro quo)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 A, Example 3 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: quid pro quo | keywords: field trip, boss, threatens career
During an official trip, an organisation's director makes a physical advance and forces himself on a researcher while giving sexually explicit details of his relationships. When she threatens to make it public, he threatens to destroy her career. Handbook view: quid pro quo harassment; the conduct is unwelcome and occurs in a matrix of power.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: supervisor touching and colleagues' gossip (hostile environment)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 B, Example 1 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: hostile work environment
A factory supervisor keeps trying to touch a worker on pretexts such as adjusting her dupatta; colleagues mock her for the "special treatment" and gossip. Handbook view: the touching is unwelcome and sexual, and the gossip based on it creates a hostile work environment.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: catcalling and staring at a construction site (hostile environment)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 B, Example 2 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: hostile work environment | keywords: daily wage worker, catcall, whistle, staring
A daily-wage worker is stared at while feeding her baby; when she asks the man to stay away he persists, and a group of workers catcall and whistle at her, saying they are only joking. Handbook view: ogling, stalking and gossip create a hostile work environment.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: refusal followed by rumours, advances and punishment duty (hostile environment)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 B, Example 3 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: hostile work environment | keywords: rumour, character assassination, arbitrary disciplinary action
After a woman army officer refuses a senior officer's proposal, he spreads rumours about her; three other senior officers make repeated advances, and when she protests she is singled out for extra physical training. Handbook view: rumours, character assassination, unwelcome advances and arbitrary disciplinary action amount to a hostile work environment.

## Handbook 2.7 — Scenario: boss's advances then humiliation after rejection (hostile environment)
- source: MWCD Handbook 2.7 B, Example 4 | topic: workplace_scenario | form: hostile work environment | keywords: boss, rejected advances, withdrawing work, humiliation
A journalist's employer summons a researcher to his office on work pretexts and makes verbal sexual advances and sexually coloured remarks. After she firmly rebuffs him, he ridicules her work, humiliates her in front of colleagues, and allots projects then arbitrarily withdraws them. Handbook view: the workplace actions result from her rebuffing the advances and constitute a hostile work environment.

## Handbook 3.1 — Who is an employer and what must employers and District Officers do
- source: MWCD Handbook 3.1-3.3 | topic: employer_duties | keywords: employer, policy, awareness, ICC must be set up
Employer means the head of a government or local authority workplace (or officer specified), any person responsible for management, supervision and control of a workplace, or a person or household that employs domestic workers or women employees. Employers and the District Officer must: create and communicate a detailed policy; ensure awareness and orientation; constitute Complaints Committees; ensure the committees are trained; prepare an annual report to the State Government; and the District Officer appoints a nodal officer to receive complaints locally. A District Officer is a District Magistrate, Additional District Magistrate, Collector or Deputy Collector notified by the State Government.

## Handbook 3.2.1 — Internal Complaints Committee (ICC)
- source: MWCD Handbook 3.2.1 | topic: complaint_mechanism | keywords: ICC, internal committee, who sits on the committee, which employers
Every employer must constitute an ICC by written order, at every administrative unit or office. All Complaints Committees must have 50 per cent women, and members hold office for no more than three years. ICC composition: a Chairperson (a woman working at senior level; if not available, nominated from another office or unit of the same employer); at least 2 members from among employees committed to the cause of women or with legal knowledge or social work experience; and 1 member from an NGO or association committed to the cause of women or a person familiar with sexual harassment issues. The Madras High Court awarded Rs. 1.68 crore damages in Ms. G v. ISG Novasoft Technologies Ltd for the employer's failure to set up a committee under the Vishaka guidelines.

## Handbook 3.2.1 — Local Complaints Committee (LCC) and who goes to it
- source: MWCD Handbook 3.2.1, 4.1 | topic: complaint_mechanism | keywords: LCC, small workplace, fewer than 10 workers, complaint against employer, domestic worker, nodal officer
The District Officer constitutes an LCC in every district. The LCC receives complaints from women working in an organisation with fewer than 10 workers, when the complaint is against the employer himself, and from domestic workers. LCC composition: a Chairperson from eminent women in social work; one member from women working in the block, taluka, tehsil, ward or municipality; two members from NGOs or persons familiar with sexual harassment (at least one a woman, at least one with legal background); and an ex officio member, the district officer for social welfare or women and child development. One nominee must be a woman from the SC/ST/OBC/Minority community. The Nodal Officer in each block, taluka, tehsil, ward or municipality receives complaints and must forward them to the LCC within seven days.

## Handbook 4.1 — Who can file the complaint on her behalf
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.1 | topic: complaint_process | keywords: someone else file, friend, relative, physical incapacity, mental incapacity, death
If the aggrieved woman cannot complain herself: for physical incapacity, her relative, friend, co-worker, an officer of the National or State Commission for Women, or any person with knowledge of the incident with her written consent; for mental incapacity, her relative, friend, special educator, qualified psychiatrist or psychologist, or the guardian or authority caring for her, or any person with knowledge of the incident jointly with one of them; if she has died, any person with knowledge of the incident and the written consent of her legal heir; for any other reason, any person with knowledge of the incident with her written consent.

## Handbook 4.2 — What the complaint should contain
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.2 | topic: complaint_process | keywords: how to write complaint, complaint contents, dates, witnesses, respondent name
A written complaint should describe each incident, with dates, timings and locations; the name of the respondent(s); and the working relationship between the parties. The person designated to handle complaints must help write the complaint if she asks for assistance.

## Handbook 4.3 — Rights of the complainant and the respondent
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.3 | topic: complaint_process | keywords: rights, confidentiality, non-retaliation, FIR support, appeal
The complainant can expect a trained, skilled and competent committee, a time-bound process, confidentiality, non-retaliation, counselling or other support, and help if she chooses criminal proceedings. Her rights include: an empathetic hearing; a copy of the respondent's statement, evidence and witness list; identity kept confidential; support lodging an FIR if she chooses criminal proceedings; her statement being recorded in the respondent's absence if she fears intimidation; and the right to appeal. The respondent has the right to a patient, unbiased hearing, copies of the complainant's statement, evidence and witnesses, confidentiality of identity, and the right to appeal.

## Handbook 4.8 — Informal route (conciliation) and formal route
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.8 Steps 2-4 | topic: complaint_process | keywords: settlement, conciliation, formal inquiry, choose
The complaint is first reviewed for clarity and whether more information is needed, and the complainant is told in writing that it was received. She is told about informal and formal options. Informal: counselling, educating or warning the respondent to stop, or a neutral conciliator between the parties; the committee must first assess severity and, if needed, help her choose the formal route, and must never advise her to resolve the matter directly with the respondent. Any informal resolution is recorded and forwarded to the ICC/LCC and then to the employer or District Officer. The choice of the formal process rests with the complainant even if the committee thinks informal resolution is possible. If she chooses the formal route, or the complaint is serious, the committee conducts a formal inquiry.

## Handbook 4.8 — Timelines under the Act
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.8 Step 1, 4.9 (timelines table) | topic: complaint_process | keywords: deadline, time limit, how long, 3 months, 90 days, 60 days, appeal
Submission of complaint: within 3 months of the last incident. Notice to the respondent: within 7 days of receiving a copy of the complaint. Respondent's written reply: within 10 days after that. Completion of inquiry: within 90 days. Report by the ICC/LCC to the employer or District Officer: within 10 days of completing the inquiry (copies are also made available to the parties). Implementation of recommendations: within 60 days. Appeal to a court or tribunal: within 90 days of the recommendations.
Not covered by this handbook: whether the 3-month period can be extended. Check the Act text before stating any extension.

## Handbook 4.8 — Interim measures while the complaint is pending
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.8 Step 7 | topic: complaint_process | keywords: transfer, leave, protection during inquiry
While an inquiry is pending the complainant may request in writing her own transfer or the respondent's transfer, or leave (up to 3 months), and may ask that the respondent be restrained from reporting on her work performance, writing her confidential report, or supervising her academic activities if she is a student. Even without a request the committee must take corrective action to prevent ongoing harassment.

## Handbook 4.8 — Inquiry findings, recommendations and damages
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.8 Stages 3-6 | topic: complaint_process | keywords: outcome, punishment, compensation, termination, damages
Interviews are done separately and in confidence; the complainant and respondent are not brought face to face. The committee decides on a balance of probability whether harassment took place and finds the complaint upheld, not upheld or inconclusive. Before finalising, where both parties are employees, the committee shares its finding with both and lets them make representations. If not upheld, it recommends no action. If upheld, it may recommend action under the workplace's service rules or, where there are none, disciplinary action (written apology, reprimand, warning, censure), withholding promotion or increment, termination, counselling or community service. It may also recommend financial damages, considering mental trauma, pain and distress, medical expenses, loss of career opportunity, and the respondent's income and financial status; unpaid amounts are recoverable as an arrear of land revenue. Anyone dissatisfied with the findings or with non-implementation may appeal to the appropriate court or tribunal.

## Handbook 4.9 and 5.3 — Confidentiality, false complaints and penalties
- source: MWCD Handbook 4.9 (3), 5.3 | topic: confidentiality_penalties | keywords: privacy, false complaint, penalty for employer, fine, license cancelled
The Act prohibits publishing or making known the contents of a complaint, the identity and address of the complainant, respondent and witnesses, conciliation or inquiry proceedings, recommendations, and action taken. Penalty for breach: as per service rules or Rs. 5,000 collected by the employer. Justice secured may be publicised only without disclosing identity or particulars. Failing to prove a complaint is not itself an offence, but making a false or malicious complaint or producing a forged or misleading document is an offence.
An employer can be penalised up to INR 50,000 for failing to constitute an ICC, failing to act on committee recommendations, failing to file the annual report, or contravening the Act or Rules. For a repeat breach: twice the punishment or higher punishment under another law, and cancellation, withdrawal or non-renewal of the licence or registration needed to carry on business.
